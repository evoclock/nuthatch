# 1 Temperature alters specificity in a host-parasite interaction

**Authors:** Abbey Ramirez, Amanda Gibson
**Year:** 2026
**DOI:** 10.1101/2026.05.11.724370

## Abstract

| 30 | The Red Queen Hypothesis proposes that host-parasite coevolution maintains genetic diversity | |------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------| | 31 | through negative frequency-dependent selection. The key idea is that parasites adapt to | | 32 | specifically infect common host genotypes, conferring an advantage on rare host genotypes. This | | 33 | rare advantage prevents the loss of rare genotypes and limits the fixation of common ones | | 34 | (Haldane, 1949; Clarke, 1976; Jaenike, 1978; Bell, 1982). This long-standing hypothesis now | | 35 | has substantial support, arguing for an important role for coevolving parasites in maintaining | | 36 | genetic diversity and sexual reproduction (Lively, 1987; Chaboudez and Burdon, 1995; Lively | | 37 | and Dybdahl, 2000; Decaestecker et al. , 2007; Wolinska and Spaak, 2009; Moran et al. , 2011). | | 38 | Despite the weight of evidence for the Red Queen Hypothesis, it has been challenged as a | | 39 | general explanation for the maintenance of diversity (Parker, 1994; Little, 2002; Otto and | | 40 | Nuismer, 2004). A key problem lies with the requirement of genetic specificity between host and | | 41 | parasite (G H X G P ). Specifically, this genetic specificity may not be robust to environmental | | 42 | change, an inherent feature of natural settings. Red Queen models were founded on "Matching | | 43 | Alleles" specificity, which models self/nonself recognition: hosts kill parasite genotypes that are | | 44 | recognized as non-self, but fail to recognize and thus succumb to infection by parasite genotypes | | 45 | that mimic self ("a match") (Hamilton, 1980, 1993; Frank, 1997; Grosberg and Hart, 2000; | | 46 | Agrawal and Lively, 2002; Lambrechts, Fellous and Koella, 2006; Dybdahl, Jenkins and | | 47 | Nuismer, 2014). Thus, parasites evolve to "match" the common host genotype at the expense of | | 48 | infecting rare host genotypes, driving negative frequency-dependent selection. Coevolutionary | | 49 | models have since established that Red Queen dynamics and the maintenance of genetic | | 50 | diversity do not require this strict one-to-one specificity and can occur under alternate infection | | 51 | matrices (e.g., gene-for-gene); however, some form of G H X G P specificity is required (Agrawal | | 52 | and Lively, 2002; Engelstäder and Bonhoeffer, 2009; Kwiatkowski, Engelstäder and | | 53 | Vorburger, 2012; Engelstäder, 2015). Supporting this requirement, there is ample empirical | | 54 | evidence that infection success depends on the interaction of host and parasite genotype (Lively, | | 55 | 1989; Carius, Little and Ebert, 2001; Koch and Schmid-Hempel, 2012; Rouchet and Vorburger, | | 56 | 2012). | | 57 | A remaining challenge is that negative frequency-dependent selection by parasites requires that | | 58 | this specificity be stable and persistent. Yet the genetic interaction between hosts and parasites | | 59 | may be sensitive to the environment (G H X G P E) and thus liable to change on relevant temporal | | 60 | scales (Blanford et al. , 2003; Thomas and Blanford, 2003; Lazzaro and Little, 2008; Laine, 2009; | | 61 | Wolinska and King, 2009). Environmental variation could disrupt host-parasite specificity in two | | 62 | ways. First, the relative performance of combinations of host and parasite genotypes could | | 63 | change across environmental contexts, such that host-parasite combinations with relatively high | | 64 | infection in one context have relatively low infection in another. This effect is referred to as a | | 65 | shift in rank order. These shifts in fitness rankings can weaken or eliminate rare advantage, if | | 66 | rare host genotypes are under-infected only in certain contexts. Second, the strength of | | 67 | specificity could vary across environmental contexts, with greater divergence among host- | | 68 | parasite combinations in some contexts than in others. This effect is expected to generate | | 69 | variation in the magnitude of the advantage of rare genotypes (Mostowy and Engelstäder, 2011). |
